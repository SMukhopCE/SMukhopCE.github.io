Making RICON map-ready

September 2026

In 2020 I published RICON: a graph of real, routed connectivity between dams and streamgages, built for the Colorado River Basin and later extended across the full contiguous US. It answers a specific, unglamorous question — which downstream gauge or reservoir is actually hydrologically connected to, and affected by, conditions at any given upstream node — using actual flow routing and dam operations rather than statistical similarity between catchments. For six years it has lived as a set of CSVs: a dam table, a gauge table, and an edge list of routed distances between them.

That's worth revisiting now for two reasons. Back in June, Google open-sourced OpenHydroNet, the framework behind Flood Hub — a heavily modified fork of NeuralHydrology that ships the two model architectures running operationally there (a Mean Embedding Forecast LSTM and, before it, a State Handoff Forecast LSTM), trained on the community Caravan dataset. It's the same model family behind the 2024 Nature paper showing a single, globally trained network can forecast floods in basins that have never had a gauge at all. There's now a real, open codebase to think about RICON alongside, not just a paper.

Second, there's a live disagreement in the literature about whether river network topology actually helps these models. Wang et al. (published in npj Natural Hazards, 2025) showed a reachability-based graph transformation — one built specifically to counteract "over-squashing," the tendency of sparse, tree-like river graphs to bury long-range dependencies — got the same accuracy at 24 hours that a topology-free baseline only reached at hour 14, roughly a 71% effective extension of warning time. Kirschstein and Sun, working independently, found the opposite: their models' learned edge weights showed no correlation with actual river topology at all, and gained nothing from it. Read together, the honest takeaway isn't "topology helps" — it's that topology helps when the graph is built to solve a specific, identified problem, not just added because it's physically real. RICON's graph is exactly that kind of specific — though the comparison is worth being precise about. Wang et al.'s and Kirschstein and Sun's graphs are already built from real river-network topology, not statistical catchment similarity; what neither tests is engineered infrastructure. RICON's dams are first-class nodes with their own operational effect on downstream flow, not just points along a channel — a layer neither paper's graph construction includes.

Which is what made the CSVs feel like unfinished business. This year I built a conversion layer that turns RICON's raw output — 1,344 dams, 1,656 gauges, 3,092 routed edges for the Colorado River Basin alone — into actual shapefiles and a GeoPackage that QGIS, ArcGIS, or any GIS tool can load directly. It's pure Python with no GDAL or geopandas dependency: shapefiles cap attribute column names at 10 characters, so the consolidated GeoPackage (built directly over SQLite, with the right application_id PRAGMA so QGIS and ArcGIS actually recognize it) is the more useful target, and reading the 426MB NHD flowline shapefile that RICON's edges route across required streaming rather than loading it whole.

That number is a nice check on its own: it's the same Glen Canyon–to–Hoover distance the 2020 paper reported, reproduced independently from the raw edge list. Here's what the full basin looks like once it's actually a graph you can see, rather than 3,092 rows in a spreadsheet:

I don't have this fully worked out yet — whether an engineered connectivity graph like RICON's is the kind of structure that would move the needle in a topology-aware forecasting model, or where dam-regulated reaches in particular might be the place a catchment-similarity model is most likely to miss something. But the data is finally in a shape where that's a question someone could actually test, instead of one that stays stuck at the level of a paper citation.
